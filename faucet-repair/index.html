What to do before we arrive when a valve will not shut off or a supply line lets go

A pressurized supply line under a sink can empty a lot of water into a cabinet and floor in a short time. The fix starts with stopping the flow upstream, not at the faucet itself.

Take these steps while the line is on its way:

  1. Try the fixture shutoff first. Turn the small oval handle under the sink clockwise. Do not force a valve that crumbles or will not move.
  2. Close the home's main water valve. It is usually near the water meter or where the line enters the house. Everyone in the household should know where it is.
  3. Open the lowest faucet to drain the lines. This relieves pressure and lowers the amount of water still coming out.
  4. Move belongings and keep clear of outlets. Towels hold back a surprising amount of water, but standing water near power is never safe.

If a valve crumbles or the main will not close, tell the dispatcher at once. A broken shutoff is a job the plumber may need to treat as a burst pipe rather than a simple faucet swap.

Find the leak by where the water shows up

Where the water appears tells you which part is likely at fault. Here is how the usual cases sort out.

Water at the spout

A worn cartridge, washer or seat. The faucet drips even when fully closed.

Water at the handle

A loose packing nut or worn O-ring. The leak runs down the body of the faucet.

Water under the sink

A failing supply line, compression nut or drain connection. Check with a dry paper towel.

Water behind the tub or shower wall

A leaking valve body or a spout seal. This is the one to treat quickly.

Water around the base of the faucet

A failed gasket or a cracked body. The faucet may be past repair.

Low flow at the aerator

Mineral scale in the screen. A clean aerator often restores the stream.

Cartridges, hard water and why faucets fail

Most modern faucets use a cartridge, a small cylinder that controls flow and temperature. When it wears out the faucet drips, the handle goes stiff, or the water will not mix. Replacing the cartridge is often cheaper than a new faucet, as long as the right part can be found. Fixture work is judged against the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) as adopted in the California Plumbing Code.

Hard water shortens cartridge life. Golden State Water's 2026 report lists average hardness of 188 mg/L, about 11 grains per gallon, across its Placentia area. Scale grows on seals and inside spouts, which is why aerators clog and handles bind. A water softener is the long-term answer if every fixture in the house shows the same buildup.

Tub, shower and outdoor faucets

Faucets are not only at the kitchen sink. The same family of repairs covers the rest of the house.

  • ✓Tub spouts and shower valves. A leaking spout wastes hot water and can soak the wall behind it. A showerhead that drips after shutoff points to a worn cartridge.
  • ✓Utility and laundry sinks. These valves are used less often and stick more. Replacing the faucet and the shutoff together prevents a second call.
  • ✓Outdoor spigots. A drip at the handle is a packing issue. Water at the wall behind the spigot is a bigger issue that needs a look.
  • ✓Shower and tub drains. A slow drain here is usually hair and soap, and the drain cleaning page covers it.

What drives the cost of faucet repair

Fixture type

A kitchen faucet, a tub valve and an outdoor spigot are different jobs.

Parts availability

Common cartridges are easy to find. Older or specialty brands may take longer or call for replacement.

Access

A faucet at an open sink is quick. A valve inside a tub wall may need access cut and patched.

Shutoff condition

A failed shutoff valve is replaced first, which adds to the visit.

Time of the call

After-hours dispatch carries a different rate than a scheduled daytime visit.
