When to call: the business cannot stay open

A floor drain that backs up in a kitchen, a restroom that floods or a supply line that bursts above a stockroom can close a business for the day. The first minutes are about stopping damage and keeping people safe.

These steps help while the plumber is on the way:

  1. Find the main shutoff and close it. Staff should know where it is. In a multi-tenant building, ask the manager which valve serves your space.
  2. Keep people out of wet areas. Slips are the most common injury in a flood, and standing water near power is a hazard.
  3. Cover or move stock and equipment. Raise cartons off the floor and unplug equipment that is near the water.
  4. Stop using the affected drains. Do not run dishwashers, sinks or mop sinks that feed the backup.

Tell the dispatcher if the water is sewage, since that changes the response. If it is clean supply water from a line, the work resembles burst pipe repair.

Commercial drains, grease and floor sinks

Restaurants and food shops send fats, oils and grease down drains all day. A grease trap catches part of it, but a trap that is full lets grease pass through to the line, which then narrows and backs up. Floor sinks and floor drains in kitchens clog with food solids and grease together.

For a main line or a floor drain that clogs repeatedly, the answer is usually a cable, a camera look and then hydro jetting to scour the pipe wall. Commercial drain and trap work is governed by the California Plumbing Code, Title 24, Part 5, which rests on the UPC. Grease trap pumping and cleaning is its own schedule, and the dispatcher can tell you how a given trap is handled when you call.

Landlords, property managers and tenants

Rental and commercial buildings add a second party to the call. These points help the visit go smoothly.

  • ✓Who authorizes the work. The dispatcher asks whether the caller is the owner, the manager or a tenant, because that decides who approves the price.
  • ✓Access to the space. If the tenant must let the plumber in, say so, and give a name and a number for the person on site.
  • ✓Which valves serve which space. In shared buildings, a main for one tenant can sit in a hall closet or a roof room.
  • ✓Work orders and invoices. Managers who need paperwork for a work order can ask for it at the time of the call.

Scope, access and honest limits on larger jobs

Small commercial calls such as a clogged restroom drain, a failed fixture or a leaking valve are handled like any emergency visit. Larger jobs, such as a multi-unit leak search or a long line repair, may need a site visit and a written scope before a price is final.

Some commercial work depends on equipment, access or building records that are not known until the plumber is on site. Tell the dispatcher what you know, and the plumber will say plainly what can be done today and what needs a second visit.
